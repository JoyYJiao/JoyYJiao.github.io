Job Market Paper

Greening the Successor: A Quantitative Model of Sovereign Debt Instruments under Government Turnover

Green transitions require sustained investment over decades, while the governments that finance them alternate in power. I study whether sovereign debt design can serve as a commitment device against successor governments with weaker environmental preferences. I develop a sovereign default model with perpetuity bonds, two-type political turnover, and endogenous green capital, and use it to compare three financing regimes, namely conventional bonds, green bonds, and sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs). Conventional bonds carry no environmental feature, green bonds attach a use-of-proceeds label, and SLBs make debt service contingent on a measurable environmental performance indicator. Under an SLB, the government pays a higher coupon if it misses its emissions target, an incentive channel for green investment that conventional and green bonds lack. Under the terms of Chile’s sovereign SLB, the SLB barely changes green investment, emissions or default risk, because the step-up is small and the emissions target lies out of reach. With a larger step-up or a more attainable emissions target, the SLB raises green investment and lowers emissions, and it works mainly by constraining brown governments. A more attainable target achieves this with a smaller step-up and without raising default risk. Annual tests strengthen the SLB further. A target on green capital weakens it, because brown governments are further from meeting it and because its step-up, unlike that of an emissions target, does not fall more often in good times.

Research Projects

Green Finance Policy, Firm Environmental Responsibility, and Stock Market Performance

Using China's 2017 Green Finance Pilot Zones as a quasi-natural experiment, I study how a policy that supports green finance affects green and brown firms. In the pilot zone regions, green firms' market values rose when the policy was implemented, while brown firms' market values fell. A likely reason is that investors read the policy as a promise of stable financial support for green firms in the zones.

Salience Theory and Currency Returns

With Xiaolin Huo (University of International Business and Economics)

Salience theory predicts that investors overweight the returns that stand out most relative to other assets, so they overvalue assets with a salient upside, which then earn lower returns once the overpricing is corrected. We test this prediction in the foreign exchange market, where high trading volume, sophisticated investors and the absence of short-selling constraints should make such mispricing hardest to sustain. We measure each currency's salience theory value from its daily returns over the past month and find that currencies with a high salience theory value earn lower future returns, suggesting that salient thinking affects prices even in this market.
